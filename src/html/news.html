Rain Continues

Published May 01, 2000

Today marks the fourth straight month of intense flooding across Alberta and northern Montana, and most of Hartshorn Creek has now been evacuated.

RCMP are aiding efforts to locate remaining residents after the body of Marge Deckland was discovered by police in her basement last Wednesday. She was featured in our obituary section.

“It’s urgent people find a way to higher ground,” said RCMP officer Marsh, “whether that be the facilities available in Banff and Lake Louise or South into Waterton.”

Despite coordinated efforts, long processing times and slowdowns at the US border have left many still stranded on highways, though Marsh again urged against attempting shortcuts on backroads.

The statue of town founder Emmet Elken is completely submerged now. Some have said that it seemed to be waving goodbye to passing search boats with one outstretched hand still visible above the waterline, while other reports have claimed that the statue had mysteriously disappeared. A fitting final mystery for our once-quiet town known for its outsized share of urban legends.

We who remain at the Gazette intend to document all further developments in this story. We’re not giving up our journalistic mission to serve our community, and we hope you continue to tune in for the latest updates on the situation and that you and your family are able to stay safe.

Community Spotlight : The Keeling Head Pub

Published October 14, 1999

There are few in Hartsorn Creek unfamiliar with this local haunt, and even those passing through are prone to drop in for a whistle-whetting. But even some of us locals might be unsure of the story behind its moniker, and in the spirit of the spooky season as paper ghosts and witches were busily erected around us, we decided to ask them about the Pub’s eccentric name.

Throughout sailing history latitude was easy, they told us. A sextant could find the angle between the horizon and Polaris, or the sun at zenith, and one knew their position North or South of the equator. Longitude was much trickier, requiring a time dimension.

Before reliable chronometers, naval vessels tried to make do with all sorts of methods for determining when the sun was overhead some known place like London in order to make their East-West measurement, such as wounded ship dogs that would yelp in pain in response to acts performed an ocean away at precisely noon.

Such was the proclaimed purpose of the thing in the brine jar, namesake of The Keeling Head Pub. The barkeep refers to it affectionately as “Murphy.” A head, shrunken in the South American style, for convenience one presumes, which would let out a cry when its body was struck or manipulated in some fashion at the agreed navigational hour.

Some claim that the head still functions and retains the capacity to scream, though none of our sources claim to know or speculate on the location of the body, the artifact having supposedly been found alone hanging in a rusted birdcage within the drowned cabin of a wreck just off the Labrador coast.

Until one of our daring readers locates it, we recommend keeping time the old-fashioned way the next time you stop by for a pint, though the friendly ambiance, and engaging local performers on Monday open-mic nights, might still make the hours slip by faster than intended. We also heartily recommend the pulled pork sandwich.

The Man Behind the Statue

Published September 24, 1999

I suspect nobody ends up with the kind of wealth my family has without a few black marks on the record. You wouldn’t know it from my grandfather’s landscaping biz, or my aunt’s charity work overseas (the latter verging on an extended vacation at this point). But it isn’t from either of them that you likely know the name Elkins.

If Hartsorn Creek has one memorable landmark (at least until that mall ever gets built) it’s the big cast iron statue in the traffic circle on main street, with those little clay-like flecks around the plaque and the feet like in the Book of Daniel (or could be that the local magpies are eating something they shouldn’t be). Below the name Emmet Elkins one notices a bolted-on section of the plaque of more recent addition, and instead of birth and death dates this portion reads “dead for ninety-nine years,” with the word “death” rendered in Hebrew as מת. A bit ostentatious for my tastes perhaps, but then I’m not the sort wanting a statue erected in my honor. (Editor’s note: Probably for the best.)

I was biking to the office this week when the Emmet Elkins statue caught my attention, with his arm outstretched in that characteristic “see what I’ve given you” pose, and decided the approaching centennial of his death makes this as good a time as any to take a trip back into some family history and share my findings with you loyal readers, warts and all. After all, Emet in Hebrew means Truth.

To our collective disappointment, the details I was able to scrounge up thus far are frustratingly scant. Through a combination of internet research and trips to Harebell Library I was able to piece together a reasonable picture of the man’s family circa the 19th century. Patrynomic name Iosifovich, Elkins had a brother Efim, and their parents Tsilia and Abram sent Emmet away with the family housekeeper (unnamed) shortly after their immigration to Bulgaria in 1867. Few legible photographs of Emmet seem to exist, though his intermittently malnourished frame certainly could not have lived up to the giant that graces our main street, but many records exist of his accomplishments.

Involvement in the CP Rail line connecting Lethbridge and northern Montana was an early feather in his cap and, alongside his involvement with Agnes Deans Cameron’s tar sands work, seemingly kickstarted much of the family fortune, though it also entailed the usual regrettable details such as the exploitation of Chinese laborers and the displacement of Blackfoot and Métiis, alongside other likely regrettable externalities. Still I couldn’t help feeling a sense of pride learning in more detail how an immigrant of humble beginnings was able to build such a legacy, and perhaps the Elkins’ ongoing charity work counted for something in the balance.

Yet it troubled me that, after hours more research than I had initially intended for this article, I have not yet managed to find even a precise birth or death date for Emmet or link any of the details of his life up with my own family tree, as though some black hole lies at the centre of it all. I’m certain Emmet Iosifovich Elkins and I share blood, yet he remains as distant and mythical to me as that commemorative statue gesturing out at his prairie kingdom.

And perhaps in the end this is for the best. Despite periodically lofty ambitions, my own flesh happens to be quite mortal, as a simple yet tiring bike ride into the office amply demonstrates. And though I intend to keep on riding, striving to condition my body and even leave my own mark in some small way, the burdens of colonialism and the legends of Canada’s age of industry are perhaps best left contained in bodies of iron and clay.

Editor’s disclaimer: we have been requested by a private individual to maintain the tradition of updating the plaque mentioned here. An up-to-date version (see below) will be displayed as part of this article going forward, in commemoration of the Elkins legacy.

“Dead for ninety-nine years.”

Obituaries : Marge Deckland

Published April 27, 2000

Marge Deckland (born February 02, 1958) was discovered by police yesterday in her basement apartment.

The precise date and circumstances of her death are not publicly known currently, but she is now the 97th recorded victim of the flooding and evacuation.

Marge leaves behind a brother and niece, and many friends from the Reader’s Club who intent to carry on the torch of literacy and wish to extend their heartfelt condolences to the family at this time.

Obituary : Amber Stader

Published March 08, 2000

Amber Stader (born May 19, 1980) passed away this morning after a long battle with spinal cancer.

Her father Dorian, recent beneficiary of a large contract payout earlier this year after his much anticipated Myriad Mall project was devastatingly cancelled days before opening due to the extreme flooding, has pledged he will continue funding cancer research as a legacy to his daughter’s memory.

“She was a special girl,” says Dorian. “Anyone who met her would agree she was one in a million.”

Amber had hoped to go into journalism before her illness had put these dreams out of reach, and she was known to encourage others to chase their own passions on her behalf.

In Amber’s own words: “Our future is never set in stone.”

Obituary: Victoria Byatt

Published March 23, 2000

Police have identified the body of Victoria Byatt after a lengthy search. She was discovered on the Waxwing Basin ‘chipping sparrow’ hiking path wearing her signature trench coat and high heels, a deadly and puzzling choice for such adverse weather conditions.

‘Vicky’ had worked right here at the Gazette up until January of this year, and her incisive brand of hard-hitting journalism will be dearly missed by our staff and subscribers alike, yet we hope this closure will grant some semblance of peace to her friends and family after weeks of painful speculation.
